This guide is for the late-season mule deer hunter trying to answer a practical question: **how should snow, stopover habitat, pressure and access change a hunt plan without turning “migration” into a forecast?** Utah DB1090, Colorado DM034O4R, Idaho 1003 and Wyoming 128-1 all reach November, but they create four very different problems. One is a seven-permit HAMSS hunt, one is a five-day fourth-rifle license, one is a mostly private agricultural unit, and one is a 90%-public Wyoming hunt with extraordinary draw demand.

The comparison matters on October 24, 2026 because all four seasons are close enough for current weather and access checks to be more useful than a summer assumption. [Utah DB1090, Book Cliffs, Floy Canyon](/hunts/utah/deer/db1090-book-cliffs-floy-canyon/) opens November 7; [Idaho 1003, Area 11](/hunts/idaho/deer/1003-area-11/) opens November 10; [Colorado DM034O4R, GMU 34](/hunts/colorado/deer/dm034o4r-gmu-34/) opens November 18; and [Wyoming 128-1, Hunt Area 128](/hunts/wyoming/deer/128-1-hunt-area-128/) is open November 1–20.

The card above represents **lead hunt Utah DB1090 only**, not the full comparison. Every hunt exists in Hunt Planner's local structured data and links below to its own profile and 3D map.

## Migration is a process, not a switch

USGS researchers following 163 Wyoming mule deer across migrations of 15–288 kilometers found that deer paced autumn movement with snow onset and remaining forage. Their traveled paths delivered 1.4–2.1 times more residual forage than simulated naive paths. A separate USGS stopover study found that mule deer spent about 95% of migration time at stopovers even though they could have completed the trip in days.

That evidence changes the planning question. Do not ask only, “Have the deer migrated?” Ask:

1. **What changed the cost of staying?** Snow depth, crust, wind exposure, forage availability and disturbance can matter differently across one unit.
2. **Where can deer pause?** Broad benches, mountain-shrub transitions, broken canyon rims and other feed-security combinations can function as stopover habitat. A migration corridor is not merely a line connecting summer and winter range.
3. **Which movement is observable and lawful?** A buck can move into a private block, reservation land, a closure or terrain with no public ingress. Biological movement does not create hunter access.
4. **What would disprove the plan?** If fresh sign, repeated observation and current tracks do not support the assumed elevation band, change the hypothesis rather than waiting for a calendar date to make it true.

Colorado Parks and Wildlife describes seasonal migration as movement along traditional paths, commonly from high country toward lower winter range, with roads and development capable of disrupting it. That is useful orientation. It is not a promise that the first storm sends every deer downhill, nor that the lowest public parcel will become best.

## Keep unlike evidence in its own lane

These states do not allocate tags the same way. Utah uses a limited-entry bonus-point system with bonus and weighted-random components. Colorado orders a preference-point draw by residency and applicant category. Idaho controlled hunts are random without preference points. Wyoming divides resident and nonresident draws, then splits nonresidents among regular, special, preference and random pools.

For the same reason, their harvest evidence cannot become a single leaderboard. Utah's newest DB1090 row is preliminary, Idaho uses mandatory reporting, Wyoming publishes an agency estimate, and Colorado does not attach a hunt-code harvest result to DM034O4R. A missing figure stays missing here.

Current costs are omitted because each state combines different licenses, prerequisites, processing fees and residency rules, and not every component could be verified to the same standard for this comparison. That is more useful than publishing four misleading “tag prices.”

## Hunt 1: Utah DB1090 — a long HAMSS window in remote Book Cliffs country

- **Current season and weapon:** Limited-entry buck deer with handgun, archery, muzzleloader, shotgun or straight-walled rifle, November 7–30, 2026. The current allocation is six resident permits and one nonresident permit.
- **Tag path and 2026 draw demand:** Utah limited-entry bonus-point draw. The completed 2026 result shows 109 resident first-choice applicants for six permits, a raw 5.50% aggregate, and 59 nonresident first choices for one permit, 1.69%. Points and the two allocation passes mean neither aggregate is a personal probability.
- **Latest harvest result:** Utah's preliminary 2025 report lists seven hunters, seven bucks, 100% success and 6.0 average days. The local profile and card's underlying record previously carried final 2024 evidence; the card was overridden with the newer 2025 percentage.
- **Public-land and access pattern:** BLM describes the broader Book Cliffs recreation area as 455,000 acres of remote public country. A newly acquired 50,608-acre Book Cliffs Roadless WMA expands protected habitat, but tribal, private and managed-access boundaries still require verification. Public-colored land is not proof of a legal route.
- **Terrain character:** The broader Book Cliffs climb from high-desert shrub near 5,500 feet to aspen and fir around 8,500 feet, cut by deep valleys, ridges and perennial drainages. That relief creates multiple possible movement stages instead of one clean downhill edge.
- **Principal tradeoff:** The 24-day season supplies time to let conditions change, but seven permits and remote broken terrain make the tag scarce and the field search large.
- **Best for:** A point-invested hunter comfortable with nontraditional HAMSS equipment who can maintain several access-and-elevation plans rather than anchoring to one storm.

## Hunt 2: Colorado DM034O4R — a five-day fourth-rifle weather test

- **Current season and weapon:** Buck deer, rifle, in GMU 34 from November 18–22, 2026. CPW classifies it as a List A primary-draw license.
- **Tag path and 2026 draw demand:** Colorado preference-point draw. The 2026 recap lists 85 licenses, 29 first choices, 119 second choices, 31 third choices and 18 fourth choices. The adult draw reached second choice: 22 of 55 resident adults and 33 of 61 nonresident adults at that final level drew. Those category-specific results should not be blended into one “odds” percentage.
- **Latest harvest result:** No hunt-code harvest result is attached to the local DM034O4R record, and CPW's public statistics library does not justify substituting another hunt or a broad unit total. Harvest success is therefore **not available at the same hunt-code scope**.
- **Public-land and access pattern:** The D-43 Sweetwater Creek herd area containing GMUs 25, 26 and 34 is 74% public land at the DAU scale. That is not a GMU 34 access percentage. CPW's Hunting Atlas, parcel ownership and current seasonal closure information must settle the actual route.
- **Terrain character:** GMU 34 links Colorado River country with the Flat Tops side of the DAU. Fire history, pinyon-juniper and sagebrush transitions, steep drainages and recreation pressure can create a patchwork of feed, cover and disturbance.
- **Principal tradeoff:** This hunt's short late window can line up with meaningful weather and buck movement, but it leaves almost no calendar room for a failed access assumption or one storm that closes the preferred road.
- **Best for:** A hunter who wants a compact late-rifle window, can arrive with multiple verified public approaches and will use snow as evidence—not as a guarantee that deer have reached the easiest ground.

## Hunt 3: Idaho 1003 — high recent success inside an access-first unit

- **Current season and weapon:** Controlled antlered deer, any weapon, in Area 11 from November 10–24, 2026, with 25 tags.
- **Tag path and 2026 draw demand:** Idaho random controlled-hunt draw without preference points. The 2026 first drawing shows 501 resident first choices for 23 awards, displayed as 5%, and 149 nonresident first choices for two awards, displayed as 1%. A future applicant starts fresh; the percentages do not accumulate.
- **Latest harvest result:** Idaho's 2025 mandatory reports show 25 hunters, 23 deer, 92% success and 81 hunter-days, or 3.2 days per hunter. The agency also reports that 87% of the harvested bucks had four or more points. That describes 2025, not the coming season.
- **Public-land and access pattern:** Idaho's Area 11 page estimates 62.04% private land, 19.01% state management, 11.09% reservation or Bureau of Indian Affairs management, 6.46% BLM and 0.52% Forest Service. No current Access Yes property is listed. Permission and jurisdiction belong at the start of this plan, not after deer move.
- **Terrain character:** The agency classifies 54.17% of the area as dryland agriculture, 23.78% forest and 20.32% rangeland. Productive edges may be visible, but agricultural food, forest cover and public parcels do not necessarily connect through lawful access.
- **Principal tradeoff:** Hunt 1003 has the strongest recent harvest result in this set and a random draw, but the unit is mostly private and the 25-tag sample is small. Biological opportunity and practical access can point in opposite directions.
- **Best for:** A hunter who can secure permission or build a fully public plan before travel and wants a mid-November agricultural-edge problem without pretending the 2025 success rate transfers automatically.

## Hunt 4: Wyoming 128-1 — public-country opportunity with extreme scarcity

- **Current season and weapon:** Type 1 limited-quota antlered mule deer or any white-tailed deer, any legal weapon, November 1–20, 2026, after a September special-archery season.
- **Tag path and 2026 draw demand:** Wyoming limited-quota draw. The 2026 resident report lists 60 licenses and 3,834 first-choice applications, a raw 1.6 licenses per 100 first choices before draw mechanics. In the nonresident regular preference pool, six licenses were issued at 20 points with 107 first-choice applicants at that tier; the special preference pool issued five at 20 points from 52. Each random pool had one license: 1,443 regular and 482 special first choices. None of those pools is interchangeable.
- **Latest harvest result:** The 2025 Type 1 record lists 66 hunters, 58 deer, 87.9% success and 8.3 average days. The legal animal includes any white-tailed deer, so the total is not necessarily 58 mule deer bucks.
- **Public-land and access pattern:** The Wyoming Hunt Planner estimates 90% public land. That unusually strong ownership pattern still does not prove that every parcel has an open road, safe late-season approach or unrestricted motorized access.
- **Terrain character:** Area 128's large public landscape supports a broad movement problem rather than a private-edge hunt. Weather can improve track visibility and redistribute deer, but snow depth and road durability can remove country faster than it adds opportunity.
- **Principal tradeoff:** This is the clearest public-land fit and the longest recent average effort, but it is also the scarcest draw in the comparison. A premium application does not make movement more predictable.
- **Best for:** A hunter already committed to Wyoming's draw who values extensive public land, can tolerate a longer field effort and has plans for both dry conditions and limited vehicle access after snow.

## A field sequence for snow and stopovers

First, divide the unit into three broad bands: likely pre-migration range, transitional stopover habitat and accessible winter-side habitat. For each band, identify lawful ingress, an observation method, a wind option and a recovery route. Do not publish precise camps, migration pinch points, private approaches, current animal locations or coordinates.

Second, collect evidence at the scale of a pattern. Fresh tracks crossing several slopes, repeated deer use of a broad feed-cover transition and a consistent elevation response matter more than one track at a road crossing. Note snow depth and age, road condition, forage exposure and human pressure.

Third, set a switching rule before opening morning. Move when the chosen band lacks repeated current sign after a defined observation period, when access becomes unsafe or unlawful, or when pressure breaks the original visibility advantage. Do not spend a five-day Colorado season waiting for yesterday's assumption to recover.

Finally, protect the exit. A late buck is not a workable opportunity if a closing road, private boundary or long pack makes legal recovery unrealistic. Plan carcass care, chronic-wasting-disease transport rules and a weather margin before pursuit.

## Best fit by hunter priority

- **For a long, equipment-specific migration watch:** Utah DB1090. Its November 7–30 window gives weather time to develop, but the seven-permit draw and HAMSS restriction demand commitment.
- **For a compact late rifle hunt:** Colorado DM034O4R. The 2026 draw reached second choice in adult pools, but five days magnify every access and weather error, and hunt-level harvest is unavailable.
- **For recent harvest performance with permission solved first:** Idaho 1003. Its 92% result in 2025 is notable, while 62.04% private land keeps access—not the percentage—at the center of planning.
- **For the strongest public-land pattern:** Wyoming 128-1 at an agency-estimated 90% public. The resident and nonresident 2026 demand shows why it cannot also be called an accessible tag.
- **For a random draw without point accumulation:** Idaho 1003. Utah, Colorado and Wyoming all require applicants to interpret point-based or tiered systems.
- **For the clearest stopover-style terrain study:** Utah DB1090 or Wyoming 128-1. Both reward multiple elevation and access hypotheses; neither offers a deterministic “snow equals migration” formula.

There is no universal winner. DB1090 best fits the point-invested HAMSS hunter who wants time to adapt. DM034O4R best fits the prepared short-window rifle hunter. Idaho 1003 best fits the applicant who can solve ownership before the draw becomes a trip. Wyoming 128-1 best fits the patient public-land planner willing to accept an exceptionally scarce license.

## Verification checklist

1. Confirm the hunt number, legal animal, weapon, exact 2026 dates and boundary in the current agency regulation and live hunt page.
2. Recheck evidence years: seasons and cited draw demand are 2026; the newest harvest is preliminary 2025 in Utah, mandatory-report 2025 in Idaho, estimated 2025 in Wyoming, and unavailable at Colorado's hunt-code scope.
3. Keep Utah's bonus and regular passes, Colorado's residency and applicant categories, Idaho's resident and nonresident random results, and Wyoming's resident/regular/special/preference/random pools separate.
4. Verify ownership, legal public ingress, reservation or tribal rules, private permission, current seasonal closures, gates, motorized routes, fire restrictions and road conditions immediately before travel.
5. Check live snow, wind and temperature observations. Use them to update a habitat hypothesis, not to announce that an entire herd has migrated.
6. Confirm tagging, evidence-of-sex, hunter-orange, carcass-transport, reporting and chronic-wasting-disease rules for the state and hunt area.
7. Carry official boundaries and current land-manager maps. Hunt Planner profiles, 3D views and the article card are orientation tools, not field authority.
