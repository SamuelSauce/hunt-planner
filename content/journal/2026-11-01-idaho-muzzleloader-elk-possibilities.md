This guide is for an Idaho elk hunter deciding what kind of **November muzzleloader opportunity** actually fits: a full month and either-sex license, a shorter either-sex hunt that reached second-choice applicants, or a 10-tag bull hunt with a much smaller recent sample. [Idaho 2130, Area 30A](/hunts/idaho/elk/2130-area-30a/), [Idaho 2138, Area 49](/hunts/idaho/elk/2138-area-49/) and [Idaho 2141, Area 55-2](/hunts/idaho/elk/2141-area-55-2/) all open November 1, 2026, but they solve different application and field problems.

The comparison matters today because every current tag holder is now working with the same broad post-rut questions—feed, security, snow, roads and legal access—under three different calendars and ownership patterns. It also matters to a future applicant because Idaho's completed 2026 draw and 2025 mandatory reports show why a generous draw result, high harvest percentage or large public-land share cannot stand alone.

The card above represents **lead hunt Idaho 2130 only**, not the full comparison. Every hunt below exists in Hunt Planner's local structured datasets and links to its own static profile and 3D map.

## What November changes for a muzzleloader elk plan

November elk hunting is no longer a September calling problem. Breeding activity is mostly behind the hunt, daylight is shorter, forage is curing and pressure has already moved through many landscapes. Elk can use broad rangeland, forest edges, broken cover and agricultural transitions differently as temperature, snow and human activity change. Snow may reveal travel and improve visibility, but it can also close a road, lengthen a pack-out or shift elk toward private ground. Treat it as new evidence, not a guaranteed migration switch.

The 2026 Idaho outlook adds a useful caution. After a hot, dry summer, the agency expected elk distribution to remain sensitive to fall moisture. In the Salmon Region, which includes Area 30A, the agency describes Beaverhead elk as difficult to interpret because animals move across the Montana line and interact with neighboring Unit 63. In the Magic Valley Region, which includes Areas 49 and 55-2, it says herds are around population objectives and that dry conditions can leave elk higher or closer to wetter forage until weather changes. Those are regional patterns, not current animal locations.

Muzzleloader-only means Idaho's equipment rules control the shot plan. For elk, the rifle or musket must be at least .50 caliber; only open or peep sights are allowed; powder must be loose; 209 primers and pelletized powder are prohibited; and the ignition cap must remain exposed when the firearm is cocked. A muzzleloader permit validation is required. Confirm the current rule page against the exact firearm before travel—an accurate setup for another state or a short-range-weapon season may not be legal here.

## Keep the draw numbers in their proper columns

Idaho uses a random controlled-hunt draw and does not bank preference points. The agency publishes overall first- and second-choice results as well as resident and nonresident splits. That makes Hunt 2138 look especially easy in 2026, but the correct explanation is specific: all 31 resident first-choice applicants drew, seven of 29 nonresident first-choice applicants drew, and 37 remaining tags went to second-choice applicants. It is not a point threshold and it does not guarantee the same demand next year.

This guide does not rank current application or license costs. A complete comparison would need residency, license, elk tag, controlled-hunt application, muzzleloader validation, processing and refund rules verified together. Use the current agency licensing pages before paying.

## Hunt 1: Idaho 2130 — full month, either-sex flexibility

- **Current season and weapon:** Either-sex elk, muzzleloader only, November 1–30, 2026, with 50 tags. The hunt covers all 176,155 acres of Area 30A.
- **Tag path and 2026 draw demand:** Idaho random controlled-hunt draw. In the first drawing, 141 first-choice applicants received all 50 tags, a displayed 35% result. The resident split was 45 awards among 95 first choices, or 47%; nonresidents received five among 46, or 11%. A later second drawing awarded four returned or unclaimed tags.
- **Latest harvest result:** The 2025 mandatory-report row lists 48 hunters, 14 elk, 29% success and 393 hunter-days, or about 8.2 days per hunter. The agency row's antlered and antlerless subtotals sum to 15 while total harvest is 14, so this guide does not use that inconsistent sex subtotal as evidence.
- **Public-land and access pattern:** The official unit profile lists 52.34% BLM, 32.33% Forest Service, 3.42% state and 11.78% private management. That is roughly 88% government-managed land, the strongest ownership starting point here, but the agency lists no current Access Yes property. Parcel status, public ingress and open motorized routes still need separate verification.
- **Terrain character:** IDFG classifies Area 30A as 75.36% rangeland and 17.81% forest, with smaller irrigated shares. That supports planning around broad feed-cover transitions rather than assuming every public acre is continuous elk habitat. The long season makes several elevation and access systems practical.
- **Principal tradeoff:** Hunt 2130 offers the full November calendar, either-sex legality and the cleanest public-land pattern, but its 2025 hunters reported the longest average effort of the three. A month on paper is not a quick hunt.
- **Best for:** A hunter who values calendar flexibility, can build several lawful public-land plans and is willing to take an antlerless elk when that fits the tag and recovery plan.

## Hunt 2: Idaho 2138 — easier acquisition, compressed field clock

- **Current season and weapon:** Either-sex elk, muzzleloader only, November 1–14, 2026, with 75 tags across all 499,166 acres of Area 49.
- **Tag path and 2026 draw demand:** Idaho random controlled-hunt draw. The first drawing had 60 first-choice applicants and awarded 38 tags to them; 37 tags then reached second choices. All 31 resident first choices drew, while seven of 29 nonresident first choices drew. Those results describe the completed 2026 pool, not future availability.
- **Latest harvest result:** The 2025 mandatory-report row lists 65 hunters, 18 elk, 28% success and 340 hunter-days, or about 5.2 days per hunter. Sixteen reported elk were antlered and two antlerless. The 28% total is far more useful than assuming either-sex legality makes cow elk readily available on public ground.
- **Public-land and access pattern:** Unit 49 is 34.62% private, 30.52% BLM, 27.8% Forest Service and 6.89% state management. Government management totals about 65.4%, but no current Access Yes property is listed. Public parcels, legal approach roads and private boundaries must still connect into one usable system.
- **Terrain character:** The agency profile classifies Unit 49 as 78.96% rangeland, 8.23% forest and about 12.4% irrigated ground. The terrain problem is open-country visibility paired with broken cover and agricultural edges, not uniform grassland. Avoid turning a broad edge pattern into an assumption that elk will be on accessible public land.
- **Principal tradeoff:** Hunt 2138 was the most obtainable first-choice resident tag here in 2026 and also reached second-choice applicants, yet it provides only 14 days. The 2025 success rate was almost identical to Hunt 2130's despite the shorter average effort.
- **Best for:** A resident who prioritizes a realistic random-draw path and either-sex flexibility, and who can pre-scout enough to make a two-week season useful from day one.

## Hunt 3: Idaho 2141 — bull-only, mixed ownership and a tiny sample

- **Current season and weapon:** Antlered elk, muzzleloader only, November 1–14, 2026, with 10 tags across all of Units 55 and 57. Idaho defines an antlered elk for this hunt as one with at least one antler longer than six inches.
- **Tag path and 2026 draw demand:** Idaho random controlled-hunt draw. The first drawing lists 48 first-choice applicants for 10 awards, a displayed 21% result. Residents received nine awards among 32 first choices, or 28%; the one nonresident award came from 16 first choices, displayed as 6%.
- **Latest harvest result:** The 2025 mandatory-report row lists 10 hunters, five bulls, 50% success and 71 hunter-days, or 7.1 days per hunter. That is the best success headline in this comparison, but one additional outcome would move a 10-hunter percentage by 10 points.
- **Public-land and access pattern:** The two-unit license does not have one honest ownership percentage. Unit 55 is 47.64% private, while Unit 57 is 30.36% private; neither currently lists an Access Yes property. Most of City of Rocks National Reserve is open to hunting, but the Reserve contains private inholdings, prohibits off-road vehicle use and has shooting restrictions around roads, buildings, parking and campsites.
- **Terrain character:** Both units are predominantly rangeland, but their cover mix differs. Unit 55 is 54.77% rangeland and 16.9% forest, with substantial irrigated ground; Unit 57 is 51.2% rangeland and 37.04% forest. A valid tag spans both, yet the hunter still needs separate access, visibility and recovery plans for the more agricultural Unit 55 pattern and the more forested Unit 57 pattern.
- **Principal tradeoff:** Hunt 2141 pairs a bull-only license with the strongest 2025 success percentage, but it has only 10 tags, a 10-hunter harvest sample and the most complicated private-land pattern. The headline is attractive; the uncertainty is large.
- **Best for:** A bull-focused hunter who accepts a small draw and harvest sample, can verify private boundaries before hunting and is comfortable switching between open rangeland and more forested cover.

## A practical November muzzleloader sequence

1. **Prove the firearm before the trip.** Confirm Idaho legality, sight it with the exact loose-powder load and field components, and carry a dry-loading routine. Muzzleloader reliability is part of the hunt plan, not a range-day footnote.
2. **Build three complete access systems.** Each needs legal ingress, current feed-cover-security structure, wind options, a shooting distance that matches open sights and a realistic pack-out. Three glassing points on one vulnerable road are one system.
3. **Use weather as evidence.** Fresh snow can reveal travel, but do not abandon secure higher cover or assume elk crossed onto public ground merely because a storm arrived. Recheck roads before committing a vehicle beyond an easy exit.
4. **Reduce the search before moving slowly.** Glass broad openings and edges where legal and safe, then use fresh tracks, droppings, beds and browsing to decide where slower still-hunting is justified. Covering miles without current sign is not automatically productive effort.
5. **Set a relocation rule.** Change systems after a defined period without fresh evidence, when private-land concentration blocks a lawful approach or when snow makes extraction unsafe. The short hunts punish hope without a trigger for moving.
6. **Keep places private.** Share habitat patterns and decision rules, not camps, access through private land, glassing points, current elk locations, migration pinch points or coordinates.

## Best fit by hunter priority

- **For the longest calendar and strongest public-land headline:** Hunt 2130. Its full November and roughly 88% government-management pattern create options, while the 8.2-day 2025 average warns against calling it easy.
- **For the most obtainable resident first-choice result in 2026:** Hunt 2138. All 31 resident first-choice applicants drew, and remaining tags reached second choices. Demand can change sharply in a future random draw.
- **For bull-only legality:** Hunt 2141. Its 50% 2025 success came from only 10 hunters, so the sample should guide expectations gently.
- **For either-sex flexibility:** Hunts 2130 and 2138. That legal option is useful only after access, animal identification and recovery are solved.
- **For a two-week commitment with more public management:** Hunt 2138 has the clearer starting point than Hunt 2141, but its Unit 49 profile still lists 34.62% private land.
- **For a nonresident reviewing 2026 first-choice demand:** Hunt 2141 awarded one of 16 first choices, Hunt 2130 awarded five of 46 and Hunt 2138 awarded seven of 29. These are separate random outcomes, not a future ranking.

There is no universal winner. Hunt 2130 best fits the flexible hunter who wants time and multiple public-land systems. Hunt 2138 best fits an applicant prioritizing draw accessibility and either-sex opportunity inside a short, prepared hunt. Hunt 2141 best fits a bull-focused hunter willing to accept a tiny sample and more complicated ownership.

## Verification checklist

1. Confirm the hunt number, legal animal, exact 2026 dates, 2026 boundary and muzzleloader-only designation in the current Idaho rules and live Hunt Planner page.
2. Check the firearm against Idaho's current muzzleloader-only requirements, including caliber, sights, powder, ignition, primer and permit validation.
3. Keep evidence years visible: seasons, quotas and draw results are 2026; harvest results are 2025. The local profile's attached draw split may still show 2025, while this article reports the newer live 2026 agency table.
4. Verify parcel ownership, legal public ingress, private permission, current Access Yes enrollment, land-manager closures, designated motorized routes, fire restrictions, weather and road conditions immediately before travel.
5. Confirm tagging, evidence-of-sex, hunter-orange, carcass-transport, mandatory-reporting and disease-testing or disposal rules. Idaho requires mandatory reports from big-game tag holders.
6. Treat success rates and hunter-days as historical workload context, never a forecast. Hunt 2141's 10-hunter row is especially sensitive to one outcome.
7. Carry the official boundary and current land-manager maps. Hunt Planner profiles, 3D views and the lead card are orientation tools, not field authority.
