Idaho deer Hunt 1087 opens Tuesday, November 10, 2026, with a public-land headline that can hide the work. Idaho Fish and Game authorizes 150 antlered-deer tags for a 21-day muzzleloader season in Area 33-1. The completed 2026 first drawing lists 420 first-choice applicants for those tags. The latest mandatory report lists 60 deer from 141 hunters in 2025—43% success across 937 hunter-days.

The draw was attainable compared with many controlled deer hunts, and the agency maps 88.3% of the area as Forest Service-managed. Neither fact makes this an easy late-season hunt. The latest participants averaged 6.6 days, the legal equipment is narrower than a modern-any-weapon muzzleloader setup, and November weather can change both deer distribution and road access quickly.

## The quick verdict

Hunt 1087 best fits a tag holder who has a legal Idaho muzzleloader, can give the hunt more than a weekend and has several access systems at different elevations. Its principal advantage is room: about 835,172 acres, overwhelmingly under public management, spread across all of Units 33 and 35 and the portion of Unit 34 south and west of Landmark-Stanley Road.

Its principal tradeoff is efficiency. A large map and a long season can absorb time. The 2025 report's 937 hunter-days are a better planning signal than the public-land percentage alone. Build a weather-dependent plan before arrival rather than expecting one familiar road or one elevation band to remain useful for three weeks.

## What the 2026 tag authorizes

The 2026 rules list Hunt 1087 as a muzzleloader-only season for antlered deer from November 10 through November 30, with 150 tags. A controlled deer tag is valid for mule deer or white-tailed deer unless the table says otherwise; the current Hunt Planner identifies both species here. Idaho defines an antlered deer for this season as one having at least one antler longer than three inches, and the antlers must accompany the carcass in transit.

Area 33-1 is not simply Unit 33. It includes all of Units 33 and 35 plus only the specified part of Unit 34. Carry the current legal description and boundary, especially near the Unit 34 split. A saved unit outline is not an adequate substitute.

## Demand held nearly flat at 420 first choices

The 2026 first drawing recorded 350 resident first-choice applicants for 135 awards, displayed as 39%, and 70 nonresident first choices for 15 awards, displayed as 21%. Combined, 420 first choices competed for 150 awards, a raw 35.7% quota-to-first-choice ratio.

In 2025, the same 150-tag first drawing had 359 resident and 68 nonresident first choices, 427 combined. Demand therefore declined by seven applications, or 1.6%, in 2026. That small change does not establish a trend.

Idaho uses a random controlled-hunt drawing, not preference points. An unsuccessful application does not build a stronger point position. The reported percentages also describe completed drawings, not a guaranteed future probability; residency allocation, group applications, eligibility and future demand all matter.

## Read 43% beside 937 hunter-days

Idaho's 2025 mandatory report lists 141 hunters, 60 antlered deer and 43% success across 937 days. That works out to about 6.6 days per participant. The same table reports 55% of the measured antlered harvest in its four-point-or-better category and 5% as white-tailed deer.

Those columns describe the reported harvest, not the age structure or species mix of every deer in the area. They do not separate public from private ground or early-season from late-season success.

The comparable Area 33-1 rows were published under different hunt numbers before 2025: 65 deer from 137 hunters in 2024, or 48%, and 50 from 134 in 2023, or 37%. That history brackets the latest result, but changing weather, access and hunter decisions prevent a controlled year-to-year comparison.

## Public management is not the same as easy access

The official hunt page estimates the area as 88.28% Forest Service, 3.69% Bureau of Reclamation, 3.41% private, 3.11% State of Idaho, 1.27% BLM and 0.25% other management. It lists no current Access Yes! property. It also identifies 61,099 acres of Sawtooth Wilderness, 7.3% of the hunt, where motorized vehicles are prohibited.

Those percentages describe surface management, not an open-road inventory. National forest roads can have seasonal designations, temporary closures, snow, ice, washouts or gates. Wilderness adds a different travel and recovery problem. Verify the managing forest's current motor-vehicle map and alerts, the full legal approach, parking, foot travel and carcass-retrieval authority before treating a public polygon as usable.

## Idaho's muzzleloader-only rules set the shot plan

For deer, the current rule requires a muzzleloading rifle or musket of at least .45 caliber, loaded only from the muzzle. It must use open or peep sights, loose black powder or an approved loose substitute, and an exposed ignition with a flint, percussion cap or musket cap. Pelletized powder, 209 primers, scopes and attached electronics are prohibited. A muzzleloader permit validation is required. The current agency rule page says sabots are allowed; older Idaho summaries may reflect the pre-July 2025 rule, so verify the live requirements against the exact firearm and load.

Establish a conservative cold-bore maximum with the legal setup before travel. Confirm point of impact after transport, protect powder and ignition from weather, carry a field-ready reload and leave enough daylight for tracking and recovery. A late-November buck beyond a tested open-sight standard is not a responsible opportunity.

## A habitat-level plan for November 10

Idaho's 2026 outlook reports strong statewide fawn survival after a mild winter but warns that dry summer conditions can alter mule deer distribution. The agency's migration research says most mule deer move seasonally and can be faithful to learned routes, while weather affects the timing. Those findings support a flexible search; they do not identify where a buck will be on opening day.

- **Build three elevation options.** Connect current forage, security cover and feasible recovery at low, middle and higher terrain bands, then let snow and fresh sign decide.
- **Audit roads immediately before travel.** A route that looked available during summer research may be closed, icy or physically impassable in November.
- **Use the season, not a single weekend.** The latest average was 6.6 days. Preserve backup areas instead of repeatedly pressuring one system.
- **Keep wilderness logistics separate.** Foot or stock access, weather exposure and meat care require a different time budget than roaded forest.
- **Share patterns, not places.** Discuss habitat, weather and decision logic without publishing camps, exact routes, glassing points or animal coordinates.

- Confirm every muzzleloader component against Idaho's current muzzleloader-only rule and carry the permit validation.
- Check ownership, forest orders, road designations, closures, weather, fire restrictions and recovery authority before entry.
- Keep 2026 season and draw evidence separate from the 2025 harvest result.
- Submit Idaho's mandatory hunter report within 10 days of harvest, or within 10 days after the season closes if you did not hunt or did not harvest.

Hunt 1087 offers meaningful opportunity: 150 tags, a point-free draw and a mostly public-management landscape. Its recent evidence still asks for patience. Treat the 43% result and 937 hunter-days as a warning against map optimism, then let current access, weather and sign—not the headline—drive the hunt.
