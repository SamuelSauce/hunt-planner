Utah buck deer Hunt DB1552 enters its final weekend today, October 23, 2026. The Vernal/Bonanza any-legal-weapon season closes October 25, leaving a current license holder three calendar days to replace an opening-week plan that no longer matches deer movement, pressure or access.

The numbers explain why this hunt deserves a careful second look rather than a victory lap. Utah's preliminary 2025 survey estimated 78.3% success, but the 2025 resident draw issued only 338 permits to 2,608 eligible applicants. For 2026, the local Hunt Planner record lists 700 permits for DB1552, while the state's unitwide general-season allocation rose from 1,200 to 1,400 across archery, muzzleloader and any-legal-weapon hunts.

## The quick verdict

DB1552 best fits a tag holder who can treat Friday as a reset: verify several lawful access chains, compare broad sagebrush, pinyon-juniper and higher-cover systems, and let fresh tracks and observations overrule last week's pins. The historical result says deer were found efficiently in 2025; 390 bucks from 498 hunters and 3.4 average days are not a guarantee that the same pattern is working now.

It is a poor fit for someone reading 78.3% as a unitwide promise or assuming that a higher 2026 permit total makes every visible parcel available. The older unit plan shows a complicated ownership pattern across seasonal ranges, and Utah explicitly makes hunters responsible for distinguishing public and private land.

## What the 2026 permit increase actually means

The 2026 permit table reports a three-year average of 18.0 bucks per 100 does for Vernal/Bonanza, above its 15–17 management objective. The annual figure rose from 15.6 in 2024 to 22.2 in 2025. Utah increased the combined general-season unit allocation from 1,200 permits in 2025 to 1,400 in 2026; DB1552's current local record carries 700 of this year's permits.

Those figures should not be collapsed into a claim that the herd suddenly grew by the same percentage. The same official table combines Diamond Mountain and Vernal/Bonanza for its population estimate: 12,700 deer in 2025 against a 13,000 objective, up from 9,900 in 2024. That is modeled management evidence across a larger combined area, not a count of huntable bucks inside DB1552.

Utah says permit recommendations respond to management objectives, while adult-doe survival, fawn production, fawn survival, weather and habitat drive deer population size. The practical takeaway is narrower: the agency entered 2026 with a stronger buck-ratio signal and authorized more opportunity, but neither statistic locates deer during the final three days.

## The 2025 draw had a sharp point cliff

The 2025 general-season draw listed 2,608 resident applicants and 338 permits, a total ratio of 1 in 7.7. Point-level results were much less smooth than that headline. At four points, 260 of 266 residents drew, or 97.7%. At three points, only 47 of 414 drew, or 11.4%; no resident permits reached two, one or zero points.

Nonresidents had 121 applicants and 38 permits overall, reported as 1 in 3.2. All 23 applicants at four points drew, while one of 21 drew at three points. Those are 2025 outcomes from Utah's preference-point process, not 2026 odds and not a forecast for 2027. Utah also warns that drawing a general-season buck deer permit on any choice uses all preference points.

## Read 78.3% beside the survey details

The latest completed evidence is Utah's preliminary 2025 harvest report: 534 permits, 498 hunters afield, 390 bucks, 78.3% success, 3.4 average days and 3.6 satisfaction on a five-point scale. The high success and modest average effort make DB1552 stand out, but they describe last year's applicants, weather, access and deer distribution.

The result is also hunt-specific. It should not be mixed with the neighboring Diamond Mountain limited-entry hunt, the Vernal/Bonanza archery or muzzleloader seasons, or the combined population estimate. A current hunter can use it to set realistic recovery and meat-care expectations—not to skip current scouting.

## A final-weekend field reset

- **Audit the complete access chain.** Confirm the hunt boundary, ownership, legal entry, parking, motorized-route status and recovery path. The older South Slope unit plan estimated 66% of mapped yearlong range as private and 20% as Native American trust land, while summer range was 76% Forest Service. Those are 2016 seasonal-range shares across a broader management unit, not current DB1552 access percentages.
- **Look for habitat transitions, not published spots.** The unit plan emphasizes sagebrush-steppe, pinyon-juniper treatments, riparian forage and higher summer range. Compare broad cover-to-feed systems and let current sign determine where to stay. Do not publish camps, private approaches, bedding areas, glassing points, crossings or coordinates.
- **Expect pressure to have changed the pattern.** A system that held deer before October 17 may be quiet after six days of traffic. Keep multiple independent areas available rather than repeating one stale route.
- **Build weather and recovery margins.** Utah warns that mountain weather can shift quickly. Carry the clothing, communication plan, lighting and cooling capacity needed for a late-day recovery without relying on cell service.
- **Report the hunt.** Utah requires a harvest report within 30 days of the season's end whether or not a hunter harvested an animal.

## Free CWD testing is available here

Vernal/Bonanza is one of Utah's 2026 voluntary CWD-surveillance units for the general-season rifle hunt. A harvested buck from DB1552 does **not** fall under this year's three mandatory hunt rules, but a hunter can stop at a DWR check station or make an appointment at a DWR office for a free test.

That opportunity is especially relevant because Utah's first confirmed CWD case was a buck taken near Vernal in 2002. As of February 23, 2026, the state reported 435 positive mule deer and 11 positive elk. Those statewide totals do not establish a DB1552 prevalence rate. Testing is the way to learn the status of a harvested animal; appearance alone cannot rule out infection.

Review sample handling before removing the head or neck tissue. If processing away from the harvest location, DWR directs hunters to double-bag remains and use a landfill, municipal trash collection or a processor rather than dumping a carcass elsewhere. Follow current agency instructions and any transport rules for the destination state.

The final weekend is not a replay of opening morning. DB1552 combines a high 2025 harvest estimate, a pronounced historical draw cutoff, a larger 2026 permit structure and a current disease-surveillance opportunity. The best use of those facts is to verify the legal map, update the habitat-scale plan and leave enough time to recover and test an animal properly.
