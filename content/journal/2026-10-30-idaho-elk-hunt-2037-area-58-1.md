Idaho elk Hunt 2037 opens November 1, 2026, across Units 58, 59 and 59A. The current numbers describe a desirable tag, not an easy hunt. Idaho Fish and Game recorded 1,372 first-choice applications for 75 permits in the 2026 first drawing, a displayed 5% result. The latest completed harvest report lists 34 antlered elk from 71 hunters in 2025, or 48% success.

The effort behind that percentage is the more useful planning fact. Those 71 hunters reported 641 days afield—about nine days each—inside a 30-day any-weapon season. A current tag holder should read Hunt 2037 as a long search across a large, mobile elk system, not as a controlled tag that converts itself.

The card above uses the actual local hunt boundary. It is an orientation graphic, not a parcel, navigation or road-status map.

## The quick verdict

Hunt 2037 best fits a tag holder who can budget multiple days, verify several complete access plans and change elevations or habitat systems when current sign contradicts an old expectation. The first half also requires a specific vehicle plan: Idaho's Motorized Hunting Rule applies in Units 58, 59 and 59A through November 14.

For a future applicant, the 2026 pool became slightly more crowded while the quota stayed at 75. Idaho uses a random controlled-hunt drawing rather than preference points. An unsuccessful year does not build a stronger point position for the next application.

## What the 2026 tag authorizes

IDFG lists Hunt 2037 as an any-weapon season for antlered elk from November 1 through November 30, 2026. Area 58-1 contains all of Units 58, 59 and 59A and covers approximately 870,665 acres. For this season, an antlered elk must have at least one antler longer than six inches, and the antlers must accompany the carcass in transit.

“Any weapon” does not remove equipment, access or transportation rules. The [2026 Idaho Big Game Seasons and Rules](https://idfg.idaho.gov/sites/default/files/seasons-rules-big-game-2026.pdf) is the controlling summary, and the live Hunt Planner should be rechecked before hunting.

## Demand rose, but the percentage rounded to the same story

The official 2026 first-drawing table shows 1,372 first-choice applicants for 75 awards, displayed as 5%. It breaks that pool into 1,264 resident first choices for 73 permits, displayed as 6%, and 108 nonresident first choices for two permits, displayed as 2%.

In 2025, the same 75-tag first drawing received 1,341 first choices: 1,227 residents competed for 69 permits and 114 nonresidents competed for six. Total first-choice demand therefore rose by 31 applications, or 2.3%, while resident demand rose by 37, or 3.0%. The agency's displayed combined result moved from 6% to 5%.

IDFG also reports a 2026 second drawing in which 429 first-choice applicants competed for three permits, a displayed 1% result. Those were second-drawing permits, not three extra licenses in the original 75-tag quota. Allocation mechanics, returned tags and the residency mix can change; none of these completed-draw ratios promises a future probability.

## A 48% result took about nine days per hunter

IDFG's mandatory-reporting table lists 71 hunters, 34 antlered elk and 641 hunter-days in 2025. That is 48% success and roughly nine days per participant. The table says 35% of harvested bulls had six or more points on one antler and 8% were spikes. Those antler figures describe 34 harvested elk, not the age structure or availability of the entire herd.

The comparable Area 58-1 row for 2024 lists 48 elk from 73 hunters, or 66%, across 500 days. The 2023 row lists 35 from 71, or 50%, across 550 days. Those earlier seasons carried Hunt 2036 rather than 2037, so they are area-and-season context rather than a clean same-number trend.

The latest year combined a lower success rate with more effort. That does not isolate the cause. Weather, access, elk distribution and hunter decisions all sit inside the result. It does justify a practical response: prepare several lawful search systems and enough time to abandon one that lacks fresh tracks, droppings, feeding sign or direct observations.

## Beaverhead elk can change the map during the season

The [Idaho Elk Management Plan 2024–2030](https://idfg.idaho.gov/sites/default/files/idaho-elk-management-plan-2024-2030.pdf) describes the Beaverhead Zone as mostly public, variable mountain country with sagebrush-steppe at lower elevations and conifer forest on higher north slopes. It also warns against a unit-only model. Elk using Units 58, 59 and 59A may move between Idaho and Montana, and seasonal-use patterns have become less consistent over time.

IDFG's [2026 deer and elk outlook](https://idfg.idaho.gov/article/2026-deer-and-elk-hunting-outlook) adds current scale. A January 2026 survey observed 5,272 Beaverhead elk, down from 6,537 observed in 2016, but the agency describes the population as at or slightly above plan objectives. It says interpretation is difficult because elk frequently move among these units, Montana and Unit 63. That is a survey and management finding—not evidence that a particular drainage holds elk today.

Use the biology at habitat-pattern scale. Compare lower forage and security cover with higher north-slope forest, then let weather and current sign decide which systems deserve time. Do not publish or trade exact camps, private approaches, bedding pockets, glassing points, routes or animal coordinates.

## Eighty-one percent public management still needs a route plan

The current Hunt Planner estimates Area 58-1 surface management at 40.74% Bureau of Land Management, 40.09% Forest Service, 13.75% private, 3.41% Department of Energy and 1.76% State of Idaho. It lists no current Access Yes! property and no federally designated wilderness.

Those percentages do not guarantee ownership, permission, public ingress or an open road. A public block can still depend on the route used to reach it, and the hunt boundary includes private and federally managed lands with their own rules.

The [current Motorized Hunting Rule](https://idfg.idaho.gov/hunt/access/motorized-vehicles) restricts big-game hunters in Units 58, 59 and 59A to established roadways legally open to full-sized automobiles from August 30 through November 14. The IDFG rule ends halfway through Hunt 2037, but land-manager closures and route designations do not disappear on November 15. Check the applicable Forest Service Motor Vehicle Use Map, BLM travel material, current conditions and recovery authority for every route.

## A November plan that matches the evidence

- **Verify the live legal frame.** Recheck the season, boundary, antler definition, evidence-of-sex rule and any corrections before the opener.
- **Plan the November 14 pivot.** Know what the IDFG motorized rule changes—and what Forest Service, BLM, private and weather-related restrictions do not.
- **Build complete access chains.** Confirm surface status, permission, the full approach, parking and retrieval authority before treating a parcel as huntable.
- **Test several habitat systems.** Use elevation, forage, cover and current weather as hypotheses; let fresh elk evidence make the decision.
- **Budget real time.** The latest participants averaged about nine days. A 48% historical result is not a weekend guarantee.
- **Keep sensitive details private.** Share patterns and decision logic, not live elk locations or precise access information.

Hunt 2037 pairs a scarce tag with a broad public-land headline and a full November calendar. Its principal tradeoff is the work hidden behind those advantages: mobile elk, a mixed-management landscape and a latest harvest result that required substantial time afield.
