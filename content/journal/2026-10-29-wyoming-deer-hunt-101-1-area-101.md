Wyoming deer Hunt 101-1 closes October 31, 2026, after a draw in which 723 residents listed just 16 licenses as their first choice. The final regulation authorizes 25 licenses in total, and the latest completed harvest report estimates 25 bucks from 29 active hunters. Those numbers describe a scarce tag with strong historical success. They do not describe an easy hunt.

The same Wyoming Game and Fish Department page that estimates Area 101 at 65% public land and rates public access “Good” also marks the license with its difficult-public-access warning. It describes extensive checkerboard ownership, low deer density, heavily roaded country and clay roads that can become nearly impassable when wet. A late-October holder therefore needs to read the access and effort evidence as closely as the 86.2% success headline.

## The quick verdict

Hunt 101-1 best fits a tag holder who can devote the final days to patient, terrain-scale glassing, prove the complete access chain before leaving the pavement and stay flexible when moisture turns a drivable plan into a resource-damaging or unsafe one. The latest harvest result is encouraging, and WGFD's 2026 forecast says the South Rock Springs herd avoided the worst of the 2022-23 winter losses.

It is a poor fit for someone who equates “65% public” with unrestricted entry, assumes a checkerboard corner creates legal access or expects a 17-day season to make low-density deer easy to locate. Historical success came with 357 hunter-days for 25 harvested bucks—14.3 days per harvest—and only 29 active hunters underpin the estimate.

## What the 2026 license authorizes

The final 2026 Chapter 6 regulation lists Area 101 Type 1 as an antlered-deer license with a 25-license quota. Its special archery season ran September 1-30, and the regular season runs October 15-31. “Antlered deer” is the current legal wording; the prior year's regulation used “antlered mule deer.” Hunters should follow the final 2026 language rather than carry the older limitation forward.

That broader wording does not turn this into a practical whitetail destination. WGFD says very few, if any, white-tailed deer occur in the area, and its 2025 harvest table frames this primarily as a mule deer hunt.

The 25-license regulation quota is also not the number offered in any single draw report. Resident, resident-landowner, nonresident landowner, regular, special, preference and random allocations appear in separate official tables. Adding one table's quota to an unrelated pool or calling 25 the resident draw supply would be wrong.

## Resident demand reached 723 first choices

The June 15, 2026 resident demand report lists 16 licenses and 723 first-choice applicants for 101-1. Dividing licenses by first choices gives a raw 2.21% quota-to-demand ratio. That calculation is useful for scale, but it is not a promise about an individual application or a substitute for WGFD's draw rules.

The local 2025 record lists 14 resident draw licenses and 660 first choices, or 2.12%. First-choice demand grew by 63 applications, 9.5%, while the main resident supply grew by two. The raw ratio improved slightly because the allocation grew faster than demand.

Nonresidents need four results, not a blended “Wyoming odds” number. In 2026, the regular preference report issued two licenses at 20 points to two of five applicants at that tier and none below it. The special preference report issued two licenses at 19 points to two of three applicants and none below that tier. The regular random report shows zero licenses against 123 first choices; the special random report shows zero against 19. Paying for the special pool changed the preference competition, but it did not create a random license for Hunt 101-1 in 2026.

## Read 86.2% beside the sample and workload

WGFD's 2025 mule deer harvest table estimates 29 active Type 1 hunters, 25 bucks harvested and 357 hunter-days. That equals 86.2% success and 14.3 days per harvested buck. The resident estimate was 20 bucks from 24 active hunters, or 83.3%, with 16.1 days per harvest. All five estimated active nonresidents harvested a buck, with 34 hunter-days total.

These are survey estimates from one completed season, not a 2026 forecast. The nonresident result rests on only five estimated active hunters. The report does not divide success by public versus private land, date, road density or weather. It shows that successful outcomes were common in 2025, not that every legal-access plan was equally productive.

WGFD's August 31, 2026 forecast provides the current biological context. The agency says the South Rock Springs herd, which includes Areas 101 and 102, avoided the most severe 2022-23 winter conditions experienced elsewhere in the Green River Region. It says hunters who drew these licenses should have a better chance of encountering older bucks. “Better chance” is still comparative agency guidance, not a guarantee about age, antler size or the final three days of this season.

## Sixty-five percent public still requires an access audit

WGFD estimates Area 101 at 65% public land and describes access as generally good, especially in the more continuously public southern portion. The northern majority is largely an alternating public-private checkerboard. The agency notes that some landowners allow crossing, but that is not universal permission and it can change.

Treat ownership and access as separate layers. A public section does not make an unverified private road public. A mapped public parcel across private land does not establish a legal crossing. Confirm the parcel, the route used to reach it, any current permission, parking authority and the retrieval path. Do not rely on an old pin or another hunter's assumption.

The terrain is mostly arid sagebrush flats and draws, with mesas, buttes, rocky outcrops and scattered juniper. WGFD says the dry environment limits plant growth and deer density, while heavy road density influences deer distribution. That combination favors broad pattern recognition over a published “spot”: compare security cover, broken relief, forage and distance from repeated vehicle traffic, then let current sign update the plan.

Road conditions can erase an otherwise lawful option. WGFD warns that clay-rich roads may become nearly impassable when wet. Check the forecast and current land-manager conditions before committing, carry a conservative turnaround threshold and avoid creating ruts or a recovery problem merely because the map shows a road.

## A field plan for the final days

- **Prove access before scouting animals.** Verify ownership, the legal approach, current permission, parking and retrieval for every option.
- **Use road pressure as context, not a route prescription.** Compare broad road-density and security-cover patterns without publishing camps, crossings, glassing points or animal locations.
- **Let moisture control the vehicle plan.** Clay roads can change quickly. Keep alternatives that do not depend on questionable surfaces and turn around before damage or stranding becomes likely.
- **Budget for the historical workload.** The 2025 report's 14.3 hunter-days per harvest argues for patience, fuel, water, warm layers, meat care and a realistic recovery margin.
- **Keep the legal wording current.** The 2026 license is for antlered deer, but local whitetail opportunity is described as negligible. Verify species identification, tagging and evidence-of-sex rules before the shot.

## Verification checklist

- Match the license to Area 101, Type 1 and the 2026 antlered-deer limitation.
- Confirm the October 15-31 regular season and 25-license quota in the final 2026 rules.
- Recheck every parcel, road, permission and access-program enrollment with current official information.
- Review weather, fire restrictions, emergency notices, road conditions and carcass-transport rules immediately before entry.
- Keep 2026 quota and draw evidence separate from the 2025 harvest estimates.

Hunt 101-1 is a scarce, high-success tag in a low-density desert landscape. Its final lesson is not that 86.2% guarantees a buck. It is that a conservative quota, substantial hunter effort, current biological context and a disciplined access plan can coexist—and all four belong in the decision.
