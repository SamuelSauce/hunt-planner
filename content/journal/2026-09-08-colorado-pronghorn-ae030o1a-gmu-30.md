Colorado Hunt AE030O1A is a first-year, 10-license archery pronghorn draw in GMU 30. The 2026 season opened August 15 and runs through September 20. All 10 licenses were issued in the primary draw, where Colorado Parks and Wildlife recorded 81 first-choice applications and a resident adult draw that reached seven preference points.

That makes the hunt noteworthy, not predictable. There is no prior season under this hunt code, no hunt-specific harvest percentage to borrow and no reason to treat one small inaugural draw as a stable 2027 forecast. For a current permit holder, the useful task is an access-and-stalk plan for the remaining season. For a future applicant, the useful evidence is how quickly demand found a brand-new code.

## The quick verdict

AE030O1A best fits an archery hunter who already holds the 2026 license and has solved legal access to several independent pieces of pronghorn habitat in GMU 30. The long August-to-September window allows more than one scouting cycle, but open-country visibility works both ways: finding animals is not the same as getting within ethical bow range.

For a future applicant, seven points is a description of the 2026 resident adult outcome, not a recommended bank. This was the hunt's first drawing, the quota was only 10 and applicant behavior can change sharply after one season of results becomes public.

## What the 2026 license authorizes

CPW's 2026 Big Game brochure identifies AE030O1A as a new either-sex archery pronghorn hunt in GMU 30. Its fixed 37-day season runs August 15 through September 20. “Either sex” allows a legal buck or doe; “archery” means legal hand-held bows under the current equipment rules. The hunt is a limited List A license, not an over-the-counter archery pronghorn license.

The official Commission issue paper explains why CPW created this code alongside new rifle and private-land-only rifle hunts in GMU 30. The agency cited increasing pronghorn numbers, game-damage concerns and an opportunity to use public hunters as a management tool. It deliberately included a public hunt code so hunters on both public and private land could participate.

That policy choice does not guarantee public access. A unit boundary can contain public land, private land, closed roads and parcels that cannot be reached legally. CPW's Hunting Atlas can display GMU boundaries, federal land and aerial imagery, but every candidate route still needs a current ownership and access check.

## What the inaugural draw says

The 2026 primary draw recap lists a total quota of 10 and a remaining balance of zero. It shows 81 first-choice applications across the general draw and no landowner-program applications for the code. All 10 successful licenses went to resident adults; the report lists no nonresident adult applications and no youth winners.

At the final resident adult level, four of four applicants at seven preference points drew. CPW labels the hunt “drawn out at” seven points. That phrase identifies the last successful point tier after higher-point applicants were considered; it does not mean every one of the 81 first-choice applicants had the same chance. Dividing 10 issued licenses by 81 first choices gives a rough 12.3% hunt-level ratio, but preference-point order and allocation rules—not a simple lottery—produced the result.

The small quota makes every percentage fragile. A shift of only a few applications could move the final point tier. The absence of nonresident applications in the first drawing is especially poor evidence for future nonresident availability: once a new code is visible in published results, demand can reprice it quickly.

As of CPW's September 8 leftover report, run at 5:59 a.m. MDT and subject to a processing delay of up to one hour, AE030O1A was not listed. That matches the draw recap's zero balance. A current license holder should use the remaining season; someone without the license should not confuse “new for 2026” with “available now.”

## Build three legal hunt systems

Use the agency's management rationale to choose broad habitat patterns, not to publish precise animal locations. GMU 30 lies in Garfield and Mesa counties, and its pronghorn country can combine large sightlines with mixed ownership and working-land edges. Build at least three independent hunt systems before committing a day to one band of animals:

- Confirm the parcel, legal approach and parking location. A public polygon without a legal route is not a usable system, and private land requires permission before entry or retrieval.
- Glass from enough distance to understand daily movement before starting a stalk. Identify folds, cuts and vegetation that can hide an approach rather than walking directly at visible animals.
- Plan the wind through the full route. A terrain feature that hides a hunter visually can still funnel scent into the herd.
- Keep a second and third option that do not depend on the same gate, road or landowner crossing. One access change should not erase the entire plan.
- Recheck fire restrictions, road status, weather and emergency contacts immediately before travel. The Hunting Atlas's Hunter Resource Report can help assemble contacts, but it is not a substitute for current closure notices.

Pronghorn are built to detect movement across open ground and can move rapidly when pressured. For an archer, patience around a repeatable habitat pattern is often more useful than repeatedly pushing a herd. Watch from outside its comfort zone, let current sign confirm use and keep the final approach within legal access. Do not assume that water, a fence crossing or a bed visible on imagery will be occupied on a particular day.

## The planning decision

For a 2026 permit holder, AE030O1A offers a long, active season and an intentionally public component, but the code's management purpose does not solve the ground-level access puzzle. Verify several parcels and routes, preserve the wind and avoid turning one observed herd into the only plan.

For a 2027 applicant, the first draw is a warning against simple labels. The hunt filled all 10 licenses, attracted 81 first choices and reached seven resident points, while receiving no nonresident adult applications. Those facts describe an inaugural drawing; they do not say where next year's final tier will land. Recheck the 2027 brochure, quota and draw rules before applying.
