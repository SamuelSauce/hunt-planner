This guide is for a mule deer applicant with a hard constraint: **the hunt has to fit inside one week away from work, but the tag, access and field problem still have to make sense**. Utah DB1019, Idaho 1037 and Wyoming 037-1 each produced a recent official effort figure near four days, yet they are not interchangeable shortcuts. One is an extremely scarce Utah bonus-point draw, one is a 10-tag Idaho random draw over 1.9 million acres, and one is a Wyoming limited-quota hunt whose usefulness changes sharply between its public northern half and private southern half.

The comparison matters on October 18, 2026 because [Utah DB1019, Fillmore, Oak Creek LE](/hunts/utah/deer/db1019-fillmore-oak-creek-le/) and [Wyoming 037-1, Hunt Area 37](/hunts/wyoming/deer/037-1-hunt-area-37/) are open now. [Idaho 1037, Area 47-1](/hunts/idaho/deer/1037-area-47-1/) opens November 15. A short recent average is useful for budgeting, but it is not a promise that a future hunter can arrive cold, hunt four days and leave with a buck.

The card above represents **lead hunt Utah DB1019 only**, not the full comparison. Every hunt below exists in Hunt Planner's local structured datasets and links to its own profile and 3D map.

## What “fits in a week” should mean

Start by separating a **season window** from a **workable trip**. DB1019 has a nine-day regular season, Idaho 1037 has 16 days and Wyoming 037-1 has 17. None requires a hunter to be present for every legal day, but each requires scouting, travel, recovery margin and at least one replacement plan. A four-day hunt can fit inside a week only when those tasks are solved before the first hunting morning.

Mule deer also do not follow a vacation request. Utah and Wyoming are mid-October rifle hunts; Idaho 1037 is a mid-to-late-November hunt. Weather, forage, pressure and the approach to breeding season can change deer movement and visibility. Snow may improve tracking or move deer, but it can also close a road or invalidate an access plan. November timing may increase daytime buck movement, yet it does not make a particular public parcel productive.

The three effort figures come from different agencies and sample sizes. Utah's preliminary 2025 row covers 40 hunters, Idaho's 2025 mandatory-report row covers nine, and Wyoming's 2025 report covers 28. Treat 4.4, 3.9 and 4.3 days as workload context inside each reporting system, not a cross-state race accurate to a tenth of a day.

## Keep acquisition cost separate from field efficiency

A compact hunt can be expensive in application time. Utah divides limited-entry permits between a high-point bonus pass and a weighted random drawing. Idaho uses a random controlled-hunt draw without preference points. Wyoming separates residents from nonresidents and further divides nonresident full-price licenses among regular and special preference and random pools.

Those systems answer different questions. Utah's 2026 resident result—2,392 applicants for 31 permits—does not mean a zero-point applicant had one chance in 77.2; points change the weighted random process and half the permits are assigned in the bonus pass. Idaho's displayed 5% resident first-choice result is not a point threshold because Idaho saves no preference balance. Wyoming's resident quota-to-first-choice ratio cannot be applied to the four nonresident pools.

This guide therefore does not manufacture one “best odds” ranking. It asks whether each tag's acquisition path and field logistics fit a hunter's actual constraints.

## Hunt 1: Utah DB1019 — compact calendar, very long application

- **Current season and weapon:** Limited-entry buck deer, any legal weapon, October 17–25, 2026. The current local record lists 37 permits: 33 resident and four nonresident.
- **Tag path and 2026 draw demand:** Utah limited-entry bonus-point draw. The completed 2026 results list 2,392 resident applicants for 31 drawn permits and 942 nonresident applicants for four. Resident permits split 16 in the bonus pass and 15 in the regular draw. The lowest resident point level receiving a permit was three, while all resident applicants at 25 or more points drew; neither fact creates a guaranteed threshold for 2027.
- **Latest harvest result:** Utah's preliminary 2025 report lists 40 hunters, 38 bucks, 95% success and 4.4 average days. That is newer than the card and local profile, which still carry final 2024 data: 35 hunters, 33 bucks, 93.8% success and 4.7 average days.
- **Public-land and access pattern:** The 2020 unit plan does not publish one boundary-wide public-land percentage. It estimates summer range as 86% Forest Service, while winter range is 50% private, 25% BLM and 25% state trust land. Those are seasonal-range shares, not a claim that 86% of the October hunt is readily accessible. CWMUs are excluded from the unit boundary.
- **Terrain character:** The unit plan describes public summer habitat, lower-elevation winter range and a management problem shaped by forage, fire, pinyon-juniper expansion and private-property impacts. For a short trip, compare broad mountain-brush, sage and pinyon-juniper transitions with lawful routes rather than assuming one elevation band is correct.
- **Principal tradeoff:** DB1019 has the shortest regular calendar and strongest latest harvest headline, but it is by far the hardest tag here to acquire and its public/private pattern changes with seasonal range.
- **Best for:** A Utah applicant already committed to the limited-entry deer draw who can pre-scout several complete access systems and protect recovery time inside a nine-day season.

The 37-permit current profile and 35 permits recorded as drawn in the completed 2026 results are not interchangeable totals. The public sources do not explain the two-resident difference, and the 2025 harvest table uses still another permit count. This guide preserves each field in its own context rather than assuming whether landowner, conservation or other allocations account for the gap.

## Hunt 2: Idaho 1037 — random draw, enormous search area

- **Current season and weapon:** Controlled antlered deer, any weapon, November 15–30, 2026, with 10 tags. The license covers antlered mule deer or white-tailed deer in all of Units 46 and 47. Short-range weapons are required on the C.J. Strike Wildlife Management Area and Snake River islands.
- **Tag path and 2026 draw demand:** Idaho random controlled-hunt draw; no preference points are used. The first drawing lists 170 resident first choices and nine resident awards, plus 28 nonresident first choices and one nonresident award. The agency rounds both displayed success percentages to 5% and 4%, respectively. Those are completed-draw results, not accumulated position.
- **Latest harvest result:** Idaho's 2025 mandatory-report table lists nine hunters, six bucks, 67% success and 35 hunter-days, or 3.9 days per hunter. In 2024, 10 hunters reported nine bucks and 28 days. With only nine hunters in the newest row, one outcome moves the percentage by about 11 points.
- **Public-land and access pattern:** The combined hunt-area page estimates 70.02% BLM, 19.03% private, 5.97% military and 4.28% state management. Unit 46 includes a current Access Yes property and a portion of the Bruneau-Jarbidge Rivers Wilderness; Unit 47 lists no current Access Yes property. Public management does not establish a legal road, open gate or vehicle route.
- **Terrain character:** Units 46 and 47 are predominantly rangeland. Unit 46 adds irrigated edges, riparian corridors and deep Bruneau-Jarbidge canyon country; Unit 47 is more than 92% rangeland in the agency land-cover summary. A 1.9-million-acre authorization is not one field plan. Divide it into several habitat-and-access systems before travel.
- **Principal tradeoff:** Idaho offers a true random chance and the shortest 2025 effort figure, but only 10 tags and a very small harvest sample sit inside the largest geographic search problem in this comparison.
- **Best for:** A hunter who prefers random-draw access to point accumulation, can scout open BLM country before November and is willing to discard most of the legal area to make a one-week plan realistic.

## Hunt 3: Wyoming 037-1 — useful public half, weather-dependent movement

- **Current season and weapon:** Type 1 limited-quota deer, any legal weapon, October 15–31, 2026, after a September special-archery season. The license is valid for an antlered mule deer or any white-tailed deer, and the final regulation authorizes 50 licenses.
- **Tag path and 2026 draw demand:** Wyoming limited-quota full-price draw. The 2026 resident demand report lists 27 licenses and 126 first-choice applicants. Nonresidents must use the separate regular/special and preference/random reports; the local 2025 record shows why a blended figure misleads, with the regular and special pools reaching different point tiers and random odds.
- **Latest harvest result:** The 2025 Type 1 report lists 28 hunters, 100 days, 23 deer and 82.1% success, with a published 4.3 days-per-hunter figure. It does not separate public from private harvest or mule deer from any legal white-tailed deer.
- **Public-land and access pattern:** Wyoming estimates 50% public land and rates access “Good,” while also applying its difficult-public-access marker. The explanation is geographic: the northern half is mainly public, the southern half mainly private, and deer are primarily located on private land overall. One Hunter Management Area and several Walk-In Areas may help, but every property has current rules and species limitations.
- **Terrain character:** Rolling sagebrush and grassland ridges are cut by deeper draws and canyons, with farm and hay fields toward the west. The agency says most October mule deer hunting occurs in the northern part of the area and that snow at higher elevations to the south can bring more deer north. That is a conditional movement pattern, not a date-certain migration.
- **Principal tradeoff:** Area 37 offers the clearest way to concentrate a short public-land plan in one half of the boundary, but its best migration scenario depends on weather and private land shapes much of the overall deer distribution.
- **Best for:** A resident who can pre-scout the public northern half, keep a no-snow plan and a snow-response plan, and accept that the 2026 first-choice pool still had more than four applicants per resident draw license.

## Build a one-week plan before requesting the week

1. **Count travel and recovery, not just hunt mornings.** Reserve time for the drive, a weather delay, carcass care and a legal exit. A four-day historical average does not mean four days door to door.
2. **Preselect three complete access systems.** Each needs lawful ingress, huntable habitat, a wind and visibility plan, parking, and a realistic pack-out. Three glassing pins reached from the same road are one plan.
3. **Match the system to the calendar.** For Utah and Wyoming in October, test current feed-cover-security patterns and pressure changes. For Idaho in late November, add snow, shorter daylight, road durability and possible breeding movement without assuming the rut will solve the hunt.
4. **Set a switching rule.** Decide in advance what evidence—fresh tracks, repeated sightings, current droppings or the absence of sign—will move you to the next system. Limited time punishes loyalty to an unproductive landscape.
5. **Protect the last day.** Do not spend the recovery margin chasing a distant buck beyond private land, across a closing road or in terrain that cannot be packed safely before travel home.
6. **Keep locations private.** Share habitat patterns and decision rules, not camps, private approaches, glassing points, migration pinch points, current animal locations or coordinates.

## Best fit by hunter priority

- **For the strongest recent harvest headline:** Utah DB1019, at a preliminary 95% in 2025. Its 40-hunter row is also the largest Utah sample of the three, but the scarce draw makes it the least accessible application strategy.
- **For a random chance without a point balance:** Idaho 1037. The 2026 resident first-choice display remained 5%, and the 10-tag quota plus nine-hunter harvest sample demand humility.
- **For the most legible public-land field plan:** Wyoming 037-1. The agency directs October mule deer attention broadly toward a mainly public northern half, but weather and private-land distribution remain real dependencies.
- **For the shortest printed regular season:** Utah DB1019 at nine days. That compact calendar increases the value of scouting and the cost of losing a day to access or weather.
- **For the longest reset window:** Wyoming 037-1 at 17 regular-season days, narrowly longer than Idaho's 16. A hunter taking one week still needs to choose which portion of that window fits the habitat and logistics.
- **For the biggest roam-or-focus decision:** Idaho 1037. Its 1.9-million-acre authorization is useful only after the hunter reduces it to a few verified systems.

There is no universal winner. DB1019 best fits a point-invested Utah applicant prioritizing a compact, historically high-success rifle hunt. Idaho 1037 best fits someone who accepts random odds and can do substantial geographic filtering before the trip. Wyoming 037-1 best fits a resident who wants a more concentrated public-land hypothesis and can adapt to whether snow changes deer distribution.

## Verification checklist

1. Confirm hunt number, license type, legal animal, weapon and exact 2026 dates in the current agency regulation and live hunt page.
2. Keep evidence years visible: all seasons and cited demand results are 2026; all three latest harvest results are 2025; the lead card still uses 2024 Utah harvest and 2025 Utah draw data from the local structured record.
3. Keep Utah bonus and regular rounds separate, Idaho's resident and nonresident random results separate, and Wyoming's resident, regular/special, preference/random pools separate.
4. Verify parcel ownership, legal public ingress, landowner permission, Access Yes or Wyoming access-program rules, designated motorized routes, gates, fire restrictions and road conditions immediately before travel.
5. Check current tagging, evidence-of-sex, hunter-orange, carcass-transport, mandatory-reporting and chronic-wasting-disease sampling or disposal rules for the state and hunt area.
6. Treat 2025 harvest success and days as historical context, never a forecast. Small samples, weather, pressure, reporting systems and access can change the workload.
7. Carry the official boundary and current land-manager maps. Hunt Planner profiles, the 3D views and the article card are orientation tools, not field authority.
