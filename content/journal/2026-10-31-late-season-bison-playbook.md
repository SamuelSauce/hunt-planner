This guide is for the hunter trying to answer a practical late-season question: **how should post-rut group structure, weather, access and the weight of a bison change a field plan?** Three hunter's-choice Utah permits create very different versions of that problem. [BI6503 in the Henry Mountains](/hunts/utah/bison/bi6503-henry-mtns/) is open now, [BI6537 in the Book Cliffs, Little Creek/South](/hunts/utah/bison/bi6537-book-cliffs-little-creek-south/) opens November 7 and runs through January, and [BI6500 on Antelope Island](/hunts/utah/bison/bi6500-antelope-island/) is a three-day, park-administered hunt in December.

The comparison matters on October 31, 2026 because the calendar is only the first difference. The Henry Mountains combine a vast elevation range with remote public-country logistics. Little Creek/South offers a long season but sits beside a bison herd affected by an unprecedented 2026 pneumonia die-off. Antelope Island compresses the field problem into a managed state-park setting after the annual roundup.

The card above represents **lead hunt BI6503 only**, not the full comparison. All three hunts exist in Hunt Planner's local structured data and link below to their own static profile and 3D map. Utah is the only Hunt Planner state with local bison hunt records, so adding another state would require leaving the supported dataset.

## Post-rut behavior changes the search

The National Park Service describes bison as seasonally social. Large mixed groups form during the summer breeding season, then shrink after the rut. Mature bulls commonly separate and spend the rest of the year alone or in small bull groups, while cows, calves and immature animals remain in mixed groups. For a hunter's-choice permit, that means a visible herd and a mature-bull search are related but not identical tasks.

Do not turn that general biology into a local pin. Utah's Henry Mountains plan says bison use habitats from grassland flats near 5,000 feet through pinyon-juniper, treated grasslands and subalpine meadows above 11,000 feet. It also says bison occur across elevations and habitat types in every season. The useful planning model is therefore broad: compare current forage, security from road and hunting pressure, weather, and legal retrieval—not a fixed “late bison elevation.”

Group structure also changes shot discipline. Utah requires every bison hunter to review its shot-placement material. The agency advises finding an animal separated from the herd, confirming that it is legal for the permit, using a solid rest and avoiding a running shot. If a wounded animal rejoins a group, a rushed follow-up can hit more than one bison. A field plan is incomplete until it creates time and space for an unambiguous animal, a safe background and a reachable recovery.

## Read the evidence in separate lanes

All three permits are once-in-a-lifetime, any-legal-weapon, hunter's-choice hunts. In Utah, hunter's choice means either sex may be taken. The shared label does not make the draw or hunt experience equivalent.

The new Utah Draw System reports **2026 public first-choice applications and permits**. The separate approved permit table can be larger because it describes the full allocation, not only public first-choice permits. Harvest results below are preliminary 2025 evidence. None of those numbers predicts a 2026 outcome.

Current permit costs are included because Utah officially publishes them: the 2026 permit is $460 resident or $4,840 nonresident for Henry Mountains and Book Cliffs bison; Antelope Island is $1,221 resident. A valid hunting or combination license, application charges, transaction costs and, for Antelope Island, extra park-related fees are separate.

## Hunt 1: BI6503 — active Henry Mountains hunt, widest terrain problem

- **Current season and weapon:** Hunter's-choice bison with any legal weapon, October 28–November 8, 2026. The approved allocation is six permits: four resident and two nonresident.
- **Tag path and latest draw demand:** Utah once-in-a-lifetime bison bonus-point drawing. The completed 2026 dashboard lists three resident public first-choice permits against 4,269 applications, displayed as 0.07%, and two nonresident permits against 3,023 first choices, also 0.07%. The six-permit approved allocation and five public first-choice permits answer different questions and should not be silently combined.
- **Current permit cost:** $460 resident or $4,840 nonresident, before the required license and other charges.
- **Latest harvest result:** Utah's preliminary 2025 report lists 15 hunters, 14 bison, 93.3% success and 2.4 average days. That is a strong result from a small sample, not a promise that a six-permit 2026 hunt is simple.
- **Public-land/access pattern:** The 2022 unit plan estimates the roughly 296,000-acre bison-use area at 87% BLM, 11.4% state trust land and 1.4% private. Those are management-plan shares, not proof of an open road or lawful entry. The BLM finalized a Henry Mountains/Fremont Gorge travel plan in 2025; current route designation and condition still control motorized travel.
- **Terrain character:** The plan spans about 4,800–11,500 feet, with desert shrub, benches, mesas, deep canyons, pinyon-juniper, mountain brush, conifer and subalpine country. A bison can be visible and still be separated from a viable retrieval line by steep relief.
- **Principal tradeoff:** BI6503 has the shortest backcountry season in this set and the smallest 2026 allocation. Its recent harvest looks efficient, but the landscape can turn one animal into a major recovery job.
- **Best for:** A hunter who can mobilize immediately, maintain several elevation-and-access hypotheses and stage enough help for a heavy recovery without depending on one road.

## Hunt 2: BI6537 — long Book Cliffs season, live disease uncertainty

- **Current season and weapon:** Hunter's-choice bison with any legal weapon, November 7, 2026–January 31, 2027. The approved allocation is 15 permits: eight resident and seven nonresident.
- **Tag path and latest draw demand:** Utah once-in-a-lifetime bison bonus-point drawing. The 2026 dashboard lists 444 resident first-choice applications for eight permits, displayed as 1.80%, and 2,347 nonresident first choices for seven permits, displayed as 0.30%. Those aggregate results do not erase the point-weighted rounds.
- **Current permit cost:** $460 resident or $4,840 nonresident, plus the required license and other charges.
- **Latest harvest result:** The preliminary 2025 report lists 11 hunters, 10 bison, 90.9% success and 3.7 average days. The 2026 allocation is larger, the season is longer and current disease monitoring makes the two years materially different.
- **Public-land/access pattern:** The broader BLM Book Cliffs recreation area is remote, rises from desert shrub toward aspen and fir, and is cut by deep valleys. The hunt boundary also intersects a complicated management landscape. Utah explicitly does not guarantee access to public or private land, so verify ownership, Tribal boundaries, permission and the complete legal route.
- **Terrain character:** Broad benches and ridges are divided by canyon systems, with forage and cover changing across a substantial elevation gradient. The long calendar may create several weather regimes rather than one continuous pattern.
- **Principal tradeoff:** BI6537 offers the longest season and the largest allocation here, but its apparent flexibility carries the most current biological uncertainty. In August, DWR reported that pneumonia reduced the neighboring Bitter Creek adult herd by almost 40% and said it would monitor Little Creek/South for disease spread.
- **Best for:** A hunter who wants calendar flexibility, will keep direct contact with DWR and can reset the plan as disease guidance, roads, weather and bison distribution change.

## Hunt 3: BI6500 — three park-managed days after the roundup

- **Current season and weapon:** Hunter's-choice bison with any legal weapon, December 7–9, 2026. The approved allocation is two resident permits and no nonresident permits.
- **Tag path and latest draw demand:** Utah once-in-a-lifetime Antelope Island bison drawing. The 2026 dashboard lists 3,339 resident first-choice applications for two permits, displayed as 0.06%.
- **Current permit cost:** $1,221 resident, plus the required license, application and transaction costs. The field regulations say State Parks may require a pre-hunt course and that additional park-related fees apply.
- **Latest harvest result:** The preliminary 2025 report lists two hunters, two bison, 100% success and 1.0 average day. Two hunters are not a stable success forecast, and this managed hunt should not be compared mechanically with a free-ranging backcountry unit.
- **Public-land/access pattern:** This is a special-permit hunt administered with Utah State Parks, not an ordinary public-land access problem. Park coordination, course requirements, daily instructions and visitor safety are part of the legal plan.
- **Terrain character:** Antelope Island is a 28,022-acre Great Salt Lake island of open grassland, sage, hills and a developed park road/trail system. The 2026 roundup moved nearly 800 bison toward handling facilities; animals were sorted and worked October 27–29 before release or auction decisions.
- **Principal tradeoff:** The island removes the scale of the Henry Mountains but adds a tightly managed three-day window, park operations and a very different level of public-use coordination.
- **Best for:** A resident applicant who values a structured hunt, can follow park direction exactly and understands that a 100% two-hunter result describes management context more than transferable field difficulty.

## Build the field plan around recovery

Start by defining three broad search systems, not three favorite points. Each system needs current forage, enough visibility to judge group composition, a lawful approach, a safe shot background and an extraction line. Keep shared information at the habitat-pattern level; do not publish camps, water sources, private approaches, current herd locations, road workarounds or coordinates.

Next, separate **locating** from **selecting**. A cow-calf group may be easier to see, while a mature bull may be alone or with a small bachelor group after the rut. A hunter's-choice tag permits either sex, but that flexibility does not excuse a rushed decision in a crowded group. Let an animal separate and confirm the space behind it.

Then calculate recovery before pursuit. Bison body size, warm afternoons, steep ground, mud and distance from a legal vehicle route can defeat a casual plan. Assign people, cutting tools, game-bag capacity, communication and loaded-trip limits in advance. Set a weather and darkness cutoff. If the animal cannot be recovered lawfully and promptly from the chosen terrain, it is not a workable opportunity.

Finally, use weather as an access update—not a movement guarantee. Snow can reveal tracks and change forage availability, but it can also close a route or make a loaded descent unsafe. Utah's 2026 field guide specifically warns hunters to check late-season road closures with the managing agency.

## Best fit by hunter priority

- **For a current, compact backcountry hunt:** BI6503. It is open now and has strong recent harvest evidence, but its broad elevation range and retrieval scale punish a one-road plan.
- **For maximum calendar flexibility:** BI6537. Its November-through-January window allows conditions to change, while the nearby disease event makes direct DWR verification more important than the extra days.
- **For a structured resident-only experience:** BI6500. It is park-administered, only three days long and carries a distinct permit price and coordination burden.
- **For the least restrictive 2026 first-choice headline:** BI6537 resident at 1.80%. That is still a scarce point-weighted tag, not a personal 1.80% forecast.
- **For the clearest public-land management pattern:** BI6503, whose unit plan reports 87% BLM in the bison-use area. The percentage does not guarantee a route, an open gate or vehicle retrieval.
- **For an applicant treating harvest success cautiously:** all three. Their 2025 samples were 15, 11 and two hunters, and Antelope Island is not field-comparable to the two free-ranging units.

There is no universal winner. BI6503 best fits the ready backcountry recovery team. BI6537 best fits the adaptable hunter who will monitor a changing biological situation. BI6500 best fits the resident who wants a structured park hunt and accepts extreme draw scarcity.

## Verification checklist

1. Confirm the hunt number, hunter's-choice legal animal, weapon, exact 2026–27 dates, boundary and permit instructions in the current Utah guidebook and Hunt Planner.
2. Keep approved allocation, public first-choice permits, first-choice applications and point-tier results separate. The 2026 dashboard says its results reflect first choices only.
3. Recheck BI6537 directly with DWR for any Little Creek/South disease update or permit accommodation before travel; do not assume the August notice is the final word.
4. Verify parcel ownership, Tribal or private boundaries, written permission, route designation, current closures, gate status, weather and road condition with the managing agency.
5. Complete all required shot-placement review and any park course. Confirm tagging, evidence, harvest-report and meat-care rules.
6. Build a written recovery plan sized for a bison, including helpers, communication, repeated loaded trips, cooling and a conservative weather cutoff.
7. Carry official boundaries and current land-manager maps. Hunt Planner profiles, 3D views and the lead card are comparison and orientation tools, not field authority.
