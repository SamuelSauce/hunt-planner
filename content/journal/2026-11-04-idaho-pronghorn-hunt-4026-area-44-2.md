Idaho pronghorn Hunt 4026 is open now across Units 44, 45 and 52, but its late timing does not simplify the access problem. Idaho Fish and Game issued 100 doe-or-fawn tags for October 25 through November 30, 2026. The first drawing received 475 first-choice applications for those permits, a displayed 21% result. The latest completed harvest report lists 44 pronghorn from 89 hunters in 2025, or 49% success.

Those headline numbers answer different questions. The draw describes who received a tag; the harvest table describes last year's reporting cohort. Neither identifies a legal approach to the 37.2% of Area 44-2 that IDFG classifies as private, nor does either erase the motorized-hunting rule in Units 45 and 52 through November 14.

## The quick verdict

Hunt 4026 best fits a tag holder who can work several independently verified open-country access plans and who is comfortable distinguishing a legal doe or fawn before the shot. It is a weaker fit for someone equating a large Bureau of Land Management percentage with automatic public ingress.

For a future applicant, the 2026 draw was modestly less crowded than 2025, but Idaho's system is random. An unsuccessful year does not build preference-point standing, and one easier applicant pool does not create a trend.

## What the 2026 tag authorizes

IDFG lists Hunt 4026 as an any-weapon controlled hunt for doe-or-fawn pronghorn in Area 44-2, defined as all of Units 44, 45 and 52. The area covers approximately 1,710,508 acres. The current season runs October 25 through November 30 with 100 tags.

“Doe or fawn” has a specific field definition. During this season, IDFG allows only a pronghorn without a black cheek patch or with horns less than three inches long. Evidence of sex must remain naturally attached as required until the carcass reaches its final place of storage or consumption, or a commercial processor; the horns must accompany the carcass in transit.

Identification deserves time. Use multiple clear looks at the cheek and horn profile, control the shooting angle and do not let a moving group turn the tag definition into a guess.

## First-choice demand fell, but there is no points queue

The official 2026 first drawing received 475 first-choice applications for 100 permits, producing IDFG's displayed 21% overall result. Residents accounted for 453 first choices and 94 awards, also displayed as 21%. Twenty-two nonresidents competed for six awards, a displayed 27% result.

In 2025, the same 100-tag quota received 524 first choices and produced a 19% overall result. First-choice demand therefore fell by 49 applications, or 9.4%, while the quota held. The resident pool fell from 511 to 453; the nonresident pool rose from 13 to 22. That change explains why the combined number improved while the residency-specific stories moved differently.

IDFG also reports a 2026 second drawing with 211 first-choice applicants for five permits. Those were second-drawing permits, not five additional tags in the original 100-tag first-drawing quota. Returned tags, allocation mechanics and future applicant pools can change, so completed ratios are context rather than promises.

## The latest harvest fell from 72% to 49%

IDFG's 2025 mandatory-reporting table lists 89 hunters, 44 harvested pronghorn and 330 hunter-days. That is 49% success and about 3.7 days per participant. The reported harvest included 39 hornless and four horned pronghorn; one animal was not classified in those two columns.

The comparable 2024 row lists 66 harvested pronghorn among 91 hunters, or 72%, across 291 days. In 2023, 48 of 90 hunters reported a harvest, or 53%, across 322 days. The 23-point swing between 2024 and 2025 is a warning against treating one year's percentage as a personal probability. Weather, access, animal distribution, hunter choices and a small cohort all sit inside the outcome.

For planning, the stable lesson is simpler: budget several days, and let current observations determine when to abandon an unproductive access system.

## A public-land headline with a private-land seam

IDFG's Hunt Planner classifies Area 44-2 as 49.82% BLM, 37.21% private, 7.66% Forest Service, 5.04% State of Idaho and less than 0.3% other federal or other management. It lists no current Access Yes! property and no federally designated wilderness in the hunt area.

Every included unit is classified at roughly 78% rangeland, which supports an open-country pronghorn model. It does not prove that public polygons connect to public roads. Verify the full access chain—road status, ownership, parking, the approach on foot and recovery authority—before calling a block huntable. Do not cross private land without permission, even when public ground lies beyond it.

The timing creates a second access split. The 2026 rules apply Idaho's Motorized Hunting Rule in Units 45 and 52 through November 14, restricting big-game hunters to established roadways legally open to full-sized automobiles. Unit 44 is not listed for that hunt restriction. When the IDFG rule ends, land-manager closures, designated-route rules, private permission and weather-related limits remain. The same three units also host a November 10–24 Smoky-Bennett elk season, so tag holders should expect the shared landscape to change rather than assume a static traffic pattern.

## A plan for the remaining November window

- **Recheck the live legal frame.** Confirm the season, boundary, doe-or-fawn definition, evidence-of-sex rule and any posted correction before hunting.
- **Separate the three units.** Build a complete access plan for each rather than treating the 1.71-million-acre boundary as one interchangeable block.
- **Mark the November 14 transition.** Know exactly what the IDFG motorized rule changes in Units 45 and 52—and what other travel restrictions do not change.
- **Use habitat as a filter, not a waypoint list.** Compare broad rangeland, forage, visibility, terrain folds and current sign without publishing exact water, camps, fence crossings, glassing points or animal locations.
- **Make identification part of the shot process.** Require a clear cheek-and-horn assessment before settling on an animal.
- **Plan conservative recovery.** Mark the shot and last-seen locations, allow appropriate time and organize help before fading light or weather adds risk.

Hunt 4026 offers a long late-season calendar and a large public-management footprint, but the latest 49% harvest result is a better antidote to overconfidence than a promise of difficulty. The practical edge is not a secret location. It is having several legal access chains, a precise identification standard and enough time to respond when current conditions disagree with the plan.
